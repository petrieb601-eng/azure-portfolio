The problem

Cross-view geo-localization models answer one question: given a photo taken on the ground, where on the map was it taken? They embed the ground image and a library of satellite tiles into a shared space, then retrieve the nearest tile. IARPA's WRIVA program cares how much you can trust that answer — so my work is the adversarial half: perturbations small enough to be invisible, strong enough to send the retrieval somewhere else entirely.

primary track

Attacking Sample4Geo

Sample4Geo is a ConvNeXt-Base retrieval model that hits 98.68% Recall@1 on CVUSA — a strong, well-calibrated target. The goal was a perturbation that leaves the ground image visually untouched while pushing its embedding far enough that the correct satellite tile drops out of the top of the ranking.

Two-stage architecture

V10 needed a manual second pass over its failures. V11's contribution is folding that pass into the pipeline: every image gets a cheap single-chip attempt first, and only the ones that fail escalate to the expensive top-5 configuration. Stage 2 never touches an image Stage 1 already cleared.

why it matters

Stage 2 converts 43% of the images Stage 1 gave up on. Running the expensive configuration on all 600 would cost roughly 2.5× the compute for the same result — the escalation is what makes the attack practical on a shared GPU.

The bug that mattered

One line, present in every version from V1 through V8, that invalidated every image-quality number produced before V9.

torch.clamp(adv.data, 0, 1) # applied to NORMALIZED tensors # actual tensor range: -2.1 to +2.6 # every dark pixel snapped to 0 on every iteration, # regardless of the epsilon budget

The perturbation was being optimized in normalized space but clamped against raw [0,1] bounds. Dark pixels were driven to zero each iteration, which meant the effective epsilon on those regions was roughly 120/255 — nearly eight times the budget I thought I was enforcing. The output images carried a grey film, and PSNR sat at 4.2 dB.

clamp to the normalized valid range convert epsilon to normalized space per channel

what it cost, what it bought

Every quantitative claim made before V9 had to be re-run. But the attack had looked like it was working — the artifacts themselves were destroying the embedding. Once the clamp was corrected, the attack had to succeed on gradient signal alone. Everything from V9 onward is a real result.

Porting to DINOv3

The second half of the summer moved the attack onto the model inside the WRIVA evaluation stack — FlexGeoApprox, built on a DINOv3 vision-transformer backbone instead of Sample4Geo's convolutional one. Same pipeline, same epsilon, same 600 images. The result is the most interesting number of the summer.

the diagnostic that settles it

Stage 2 recovered 99 of 230 Stage 1 failures on Sample4Geo — but only 2 of 314 on WRIVA. If the DINOv3 failures were a budget problem, escalating to 300 iterations and top-5 chip averaging would have converted a comparable share. It converted 0.6%. These images are not underattacked; the transformer's representation is genuinely harder to displace.

finding

Architecture matters

A 30-point drop from the same attack at the same epsilon. Transformer backbones are meaningfully more robust to L∞ embedding attacks than ConvNeXt on this task.

78.2% → 48.0%

Why the port was hard

  1. Normalization is not portable Having been burned once by a clamp-space mismatch, every preprocessing constant on the new target got verified against the model's own transform pipeline before a single attack ran.
  2. Patch tokens vs. convolutional features A ViT aggregates patch tokens through attention rather than local receptive fields, so perturbation energy shaped against a CNN does not land where it needs to. DEV-002 falling from 61% to 14% is that mismatch at its worst.
  3. The chip cache has to be rebuilt Cached target embeddings are model-specific. The entire top-K cache regenerates against the new encoder before retargeting works at all.

Reading the source

The full attack script is on this site — roughly 400 lines covering model loading, the chip cache, momentum PGD under EOT, the hierarchical targeting fallback, and checkpointed resume across both stages. A few things worth looking at:

  1. run_pgd() — the normalization fix in practice Epsilon and alpha are converted to normalized space per channel (EPS_NORM = EPSILON / STD), and the final clamp targets NORM_MIN/NORM_MAX rather than [0,1]. This is the corrected form of the bug that invalidated V1–V8.
  2. hierarchical_attack() — cross-site first, same-site as fallback Every image gets attacked toward a different site first. Only if that fails does it retry toward the same site, which is why cross-site flips dominate the results. The margin sign decides which phase gets recorded.
  3. Momentum carried across stages Stage 1's accumulated momentum tensor is checkpointed to disk and replayed as the warm start for Stage 2, so escalation resumes from where the cheap attempt gave up instead of restarting cold.
  4. The loss is a push–pull Negative cosine similarity to the wrong target, plus a 0.1-weighted positive term on the correct one — the perturbation is simultaneously pulled toward the decoy and pushed off the truth.
  5. DINOv3 needs 224, not 384 One of the porting details that has to be right before anything else works. The dummy-tensor helpers exist because the model's forward pass requires both branches even when only one embedding is wanted.

analysis

What resistance actually is

Two experiments came out of asking why 131 images would not flip. Both point the same direction: vulnerability is a property of how the model encodes a place, not of what the photograph looks like.

Vulnerability is a source-site property

If a site's flip rate came from the source–target pairing, changing the target should change the rate. It doesn't.

cross-target experiment · same source, different satellite target
SourceOriginal targetFlip %New targetFlip %
DEV-001DEV-003 sat60.2%DEV-002 sat59.5%
DEV-002DEV-003 sat10.5%DEV-001 sat2.5%
DEV-003DEV-001 sat8.0%DEV-002 sat13.0%
finding

DEV-001 flips at roughly 60% regardless of what it is being pushed toward. Swap the target entirely and the rate moves by 0.7 points. Whatever makes a site attackable lives in how the model encodes the source imagery, not in the geometry between source and destination.

site character vs. embedding strength
SiteVisual characterEmbeddingV11 flip rate
DEV-001Generic suburbs — flat concrete, uniform grass, standard streetsWeak, diffuse91.5%
DEV-002Irregular brick buildings in autumnStrong, tight61.0%
DEV-003Irregular buildings with unusual statuesVery strong82.0%

A place with no distinguishing features gives the model nothing to hold onto — the embedding is diffuse, and a small push moves it a long way. Distinctive architecture produces a tight, confident embedding that resists displacement.

Visual content does not predict resistance

The obvious hypothesis is that resistant images are visually busier — more texture, more edges, more entropy for the perturbation to hide in. Measured directly, that hypothesis fails.

key finding

Every pixel-space measure separates the two groups by under 3%. The two measures that do separate them — gradient norm and margin shift — are both properties of the model's response, not the image. Resistance lives in the embedding representation. You cannot look at a photograph and predict whether it will survive the attack.

Across the earlier 1200-image full-dataset run, every single image showed a positive margin shift — the attack always moves the embedding in the intended direction, including on images it fails to flip. 84 of those came within −0.1 of the decision boundary. Failure here is a matter of distance, not of direction.
